## What it is

Swix is a ticketing platform where the resale rules are enforced by the
contract rather than by a terms-of-service page. An artist sets a maximum
resale price and a royalty they earn on every resale, and those two rules
execute automatically on-chain — there is no policy to appeal to and nobody to
enforce it after the fact.

It is a work in progress. The contracts are deployed and working on a testnet,
the marketplace frontend is live, and the parts I would want solved before
calling it real are mainstream payments and a move to a cheaper chain.

## The idea: rules that execute

The problem with ticket resale is not that it happens — it is that once a
ticket leaves the original platform, the artist has no say in what happens to
it and sees none of the money. A ticket sold for face value and resold for ten
times that is a transfer of value away from the person who created the demand.

So the two rules that matter are a **price cap** and a **royalty**, and both
are stored in the contract at deployment:

- **Resale cap** — 20% above face value by default. A £50 ticket cannot be
  listed above £60. The contract rejects the listing; it is not a warning.
- **Royalty** — 5% of every resale, paid to the promoter's wallet as part of
  the same transaction.

Both are adjustable by the promoter through `setResaleCap()` and
`setRoyaltyPercent()`, gated behind a role so only they can change them. That
adjustability is the part I think actually matters. A stadium act might set 0%
and make tickets non-transferable; an indie band might set 30% to encourage an
active resale market. The same contract serves both because the number is a
parameter, not a constant.

Because face value is stored per ticket at mint, the cap applies per ticket
rather than per event — a £50 general admission ticket and a £100 VIP ticket
get their own ceilings automatically.

## How the two contracts fit together

**TicketNFT** is the ownership layer — an ERC-721 where each token carries its
face value, seat, tier, and used status. It also restricts transfers: a ticket
can only move through the official marketplace. That restriction is what makes
the rest enforceable, because a ticket that can be sent anywhere is a ticket
whose resale rules are optional.

**Marketplace** sits on top and handles everything after the first sale —
listing, buying, cancelling — and is where the cap and the royalty split are
applied.

Neither works properly alone, which turned out to matter more than I expected.

## Proving it worked

Thirteen tests pass against the contracts, but the run I actually trusted was
the end-to-end one on Sepolia: mint a ticket, list it for resale, buy it from a
different wallet, and confirm where the money went. All four transactions
confirmed, and the royalty split came out exact — 0.0005 ETH to the promoter
and 0.0095 ETH to the seller on a 0.01 ETH sale.

That was the moment the idea stopped being theoretical. The rules held under
real conditions, against real wallets, and every transaction is permanently
recorded and publicly checkable.

## What I learned

- **Immutability is a design constraint, not a footnote.** I deployed
  TicketNFT, then realised it was missing the function that wires the
  marketplace into the transfer restriction. There is no patching a live
  contract — the fix was a fresh deployment and migrating everything to the new
  address. I now spend much longer on an interface before deploying it.
- **The interesting decisions were about defaults, not code.** Choosing 20% and
  5% took longer than implementing them. Too low and resellers have no reason
  to use the platform; too high and the cap stops protecting fans.
- **The blockchain part is the part users should never see.** Every design
  decision since has been about hiding it — showing prices in pounds rather
  than ETH, and looking at email login and card payments so a fan can buy a
  ticket without knowing what a wallet is. A ticketing product that requires
  understanding gas fees is not a ticketing product.
- **Gas costs change the architecture.** On Ethereum mainnet, fees on a £30
  ticket can approach the price of the ticket. That pushed the whole project
  toward a cheaper chain, which is a plumbing decision that turns out to
  determine whether the product is viable at all.
