## What it is

Swix is a ticketing platform where the resale rules are enforced by the
contract itself rather than by a terms of service page nobody reads. A ticket
can't be listed above a set percentage of its face value, and a cut of every
resale goes to the promoter automatically. There is no policy to appeal to,
the transaction simply reverts.

It's still a work in progress. Both contracts are deployed and working on a
testnet with a live marketplace frontend on top of them, however the parts I'd
want solved before calling it real are mainstream payments and a move to a
cheaper chain.

## The rule that makes everything else possible

The hard part of ticket resale isn't the pricing, it's that once a ticket can
move directly from one wallet to another, every other rule becomes optional. A
price cap you can route around isn't really a cap.

So TicketNFT overrides _update(), which is the function every mint, transfer,
and burn passes through in OpenZeppelin v5, and blocks any transfer between two
real addresses unless the marketplace contract is the one calling it. Minting
and burning pass through untouched, wallet to wallet transfers revert, and the
marketplace becomes the only path a ticket can take between owners. That single
restriction is what gives every other rule its teeth.

## What the marketplace enforces

Listing a ticket checks that you own it, that it hasn't already been scanned at
a gate, and that the price doesn't exceed 120% of that specific ticket's face
value. Since face value is stored per ticket at mint, the ceiling is calculated
per ticket instead of per event, so a $50 general admission and a $100 VIP each
get their own cap without any extra bookkeeping.

Buying splits the payment in the same transaction that moves the ticket, 5% to
the promoter and the rest to the seller. The promoter never has to invoice
anyone or wait on a payout, since the split isn't a separate step.

Both percentages are currently constants, fixed at 120 and 5 for every event,
and that's the clearest limitation in the design as it stands. Making them per
event parameters set at deployment is the obvious next change, and it's what
would let the same contract serve an artist who wants no resale at all and one
who wants an active secondary market.

## Things I got right by getting them wrong first

A few decisions in these contracts are there because the obvious version had a
problem.

The listing is deleted before any money or tokens move. Doing the transfers
first leaves a window where a malicious contract can call back into buyTicket
before the first call has finished and drain the sale.

Payouts use call rather than transfer, since transfer forwards a fixed 2300 gas
stipend that quietly fails when the recipient is a contract wallet instead of a
plain address, which promoters increasingly are.

Approval is checked when a ticket is listed rather than when it's bought.
Functionally it could wait, however then the failure lands on the buyer for
something the seller forgot to do, and an error should go to the person who can
actually fix it.

Batch minting validates every array length before it writes anything, so a
mismatched upload reverts as a whole instead of leaving half an event minted.

Thirty eight tests cover the two contracts, however the run I actually trusted
was the end to end one on Sepolia, where I minted a ticket, listed it, bought
it from a second wallet, and checked where the money landed. The royalty split
came out exact, 0.0005 ETH to the promoter and 0.0095 to the seller on a 0.01
ETH sale.

## What I walked away with

- I deployed TicketNFT and then realized it had no way to point at the
  marketplace contract I wrote afterward. There is no patching a live contract,
  so the fix was a fresh deployment and moving everything over to the new
  address. Since then I spend far longer on an interface before it goes on
  chain.
- Minting tickets, scanning them at a gate, and administering the contract are
  three genuinely different powers, and collapsing them into one owner address
  would have meant handing a gate scanner the ability to mint tickets.
- The blockchain part is the part a user should never see. Every design
  decision since has been about hiding it, showing prices in dollars instead of
  ETH and looking at email login and card payments so a fan never has to learn
  what a wallet is.
- Gas costs decide the architecture. Fees on a $30 ticket can approach the
  price of the ticket itself on Ethereum mainnet, which pushed the whole
  project toward a cheaper chain before much else was settled.
