## Overview

Swix is a ticketing platform where resale rules are enforced by the contract
rather than by policy. A ticket cannot be listed above a set percentage of its
face value, and a share of every resale routes to the promoter automatically.
There is no policy to appeal to. The transaction reverts.

Still in progress. Both contracts are deployed and working on a testnet with a
live marketplace frontend on top of them. Mainstream payments and a move to a
cheaper chain are what I would want solved before calling it real.

## The transfer restriction

Once a ticket can move directly between wallets, every other rule becomes
optional. A price cap that can be routed around is not a cap.

TicketNFT overrides `_update()`, the function every mint, transfer, and burn
passes through in OpenZeppelin v5, and blocks transfers between two real
addresses unless the marketplace contract is the caller. Minting and burning
pass through untouched. Wallet-to-wallet transfers revert. The marketplace
becomes the only path a ticket can take between owners, which is what gives
every other rule its authority.

## What the marketplace enforces

Listing a ticket verifies ownership, confirms the ticket has not already been
scanned at a gate, and rejects any price above 120% of that ticket's face
value. Face value is stored per ticket at mint, so the ceiling is calculated
per ticket rather than per event. A $50 general admission and a $100 VIP each
carry their own cap with no additional bookkeeping.

Purchase splits payment in the same transaction that transfers the ticket, 5%
to the promoter and the remainder to the seller. No invoicing and no payout
delay, because the split is not a separate step.

Both percentages are constants, fixed at 120 and 5 across every event. That is
the clearest limitation in the design as it stands. Making them per-event
parameters set at deployment is the obvious next change, and it would let one
contract serve an artist who wants no resale alongside one who wants an active
secondary market.

## Decisions that came from getting it wrong first

Several choices in these contracts exist because the obvious version had a
problem.

The listing is deleted before any tokens or funds move. Transferring first
leaves a window for a malicious contract to call back into `buyTicket` before
the first call completes and drain the sale.

Payouts use `call` rather than `transfer`. Solidity's `transfer` forwards a
fixed 2300 gas stipend that fails silently when the recipient is a contract
wallet rather than a plain address, which promoters increasingly are.

Approval is checked at listing rather than at purchase. It could wait, but then
the failure lands on the buyer for something the seller neglected to do. The
error belongs with the person who can fix it.

Batch minting validates every array length before writing state, so a
mismatched upload reverts whole instead of leaving an event half minted.

Thirty-eight tests cover the two contracts. The run I trusted most was the
end-to-end test on Sepolia: mint a ticket, list it, buy it from a second
wallet, confirm where the funds landed. The royalty split came out exact at
0.0005 ETH to the promoter and 0.0095 to the seller on a 0.01 ETH sale.

## What I learned

- I deployed TicketNFT before realizing it had no way to reference the
  marketplace contract I wrote afterward. Live contracts cannot be patched, so
  the fix was a fresh deployment and a full migration to the new address. I now
  spend considerably longer on an interface before it goes on chain.
- Minting, gate scanning, and administration are three distinct powers.
  Collapsing them into a single owner address would have given a gate scanner
  the ability to mint tickets.
- The blockchain layer is the part a user should never see. Every decision
  since has been about hiding it, with prices shown in dollars rather than ETH
  and email login and card payments so a fan never learns what a wallet is.
- Gas costs determine the architecture. Fees on a $30 ticket can approach the
  price of the ticket on Ethereum mainnet, which pushed the project toward a
  cheaper chain before much else was settled.
