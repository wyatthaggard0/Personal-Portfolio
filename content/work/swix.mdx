## What it is

Swix is a ticketing platform where the resale rules are enforced by the
contract rather than by a terms-of-service page. A ticket cannot be resold
above a set percentage of its face value, and a cut of every resale goes to the
promoter automatically — not because a policy says so, but because the
transaction reverts otherwise.

It is a work in progress. Both contracts are deployed and working on a testnet
with a live marketplace frontend on top. The parts I would want solved before
calling it real are mainstream payments and a move to a cheaper chain.

## The rule that makes the rest possible

The hard part of ticket resale is not pricing — it is that once a ticket can
move wallet to wallet, every other rule becomes optional. A cap you can route
around is not a cap.

So `TicketNFT` overrides `_update()`, the function every mint, burn, and
transfer flows through in OpenZeppelin v5, and blocks any transfer where both
sides are real addresses unless the caller is the official marketplace:

- Minting and burning pass through untouched
- Wallet-to-wallet transfers revert
- Only the marketplace contract can move a ticket between owners

That single restriction is what gives the marketplace contract its authority.
Everything else — the price cap, the royalty split — works because there is no
path around it.

## What the marketplace enforces

`Marketplace` handles everything after the first sale. Listing a ticket checks
that you own it, that it has not already been scanned at a gate, and that the
price does not exceed **120% of that ticket's face value**. Because face value
is stored per ticket at mint time, the ceiling is calculated per ticket rather
than per event — a £50 general admission and a £100 VIP each get their own,
with no extra bookkeeping.

Buying splits the payment in the same transaction that moves the ticket: **5%
to the promoter, the remainder to the seller**. The promoter does not invoice
anyone or wait for a payout, because the split is not a separate step.

Both percentages are currently `constant` — fixed at 120 and 5 for every event.
That is the clearest limitation in the design as it stands. Making them
per-event parameters set at deployment is the obvious next change, and it is
what would let one contract serve an artist who wants no resale at all and one
who wants an active secondary market.

## Details I got right by getting them wrong first

A few things in the contracts are there because the naive version had a
problem:

- **The listing is deleted before any money or tokens move.** Doing the
  transfers first leaves a window where a malicious contract can call back into
  `buyTicket` before the first call finishes and drain the sale. Writing state
  before external calls closes it.
- **Payouts use `call` rather than `transfer`.** Solidity's `transfer` forwards
  a fixed 2300 gas stipend, which silently fails when the recipient is a
  contract wallet rather than a plain address — which promoters increasingly
  are.
- **Approval is checked when listing, not when buying.** Functionally it could
  wait, but then the failure lands on the buyer for something the seller forgot
  to do. Checking early puts the error in front of the person who can fix it.
- **Batch minting validates every array length before writing anything,** so a
  mismatched upload reverts whole rather than leaving a half-minted event.

Thirty-eight tests cover the two contracts. The run I actually trusted was the
end-to-end one on Sepolia — mint, list, buy from a second wallet, confirm where
the money landed. The royalty split came out exact: 0.0005 ETH to the promoter
and 0.0095 to the seller on a 0.01 ETH sale.

## What I learned

- **Immutability is a design constraint, not a footnote.** I deployed TicketNFT
  and then realised it had no way to point at the marketplace that was written
  afterward. There is no patching a live contract — the fix was a fresh
  deployment and moving everything to the new address. I spend much longer on
  an interface now before it goes on-chain.
- **Access control is a modelling exercise.** Minting, scanning at a gate, and
  administering the contract are three genuinely different powers, and
  collapsing them into one owner address would have meant handing a gate
  scanner the ability to mint tickets.
- **The blockchain part is the part users should never see.** Every design
  decision since has been about hiding it — prices in pounds rather than ETH,
  and looking at email login and card payments so a fan never learns what a
  wallet is.
- **Gas costs decide the architecture.** Fees on a £30 ticket can approach the
  price of the ticket on Ethereum mainnet, which pushed the whole project toward
  a cheaper chain. A plumbing decision that determines whether the product is
  viable at all.
