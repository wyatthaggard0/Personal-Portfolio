## What it is

A fraud classifier trained on the IEEE-CIS Fraud Detection dataset, wrapped in
a dashboard that scores an individual transaction and shows which features
drove the score. Built as a class project using course resources; the
deployment, the interface, and the explainability layer were mine.

I trained four models and compared them on held-out ROC-AUC — logistic
regression, random forest, XGBoost, and Gaussian naive Bayes. Tuned logistic
regression won at **80.9%**, which was not the result I expected. The tree
ensembles were supposed to be the interesting ones.

## Reading the numbers honestly

The headline metric flatters the model. The rest of the table does not, and
that gap is the most useful thing I got out of this project.

At the chosen threshold the model catches **67.9% of fraudulent
transactions** — about **63.4% of total fraud value**, or $5,763.84 of the
$9,090.46 of fraud in the test set. That sounds reasonable until you look at
precision: **6.91%**. Roughly nineteen of every twenty transactions the model
flags are legitimate. It raised **485 false alarms** to catch what it caught.

That is not a bug in the model so much as the shape of the problem. Fraud is
rare, so a classifier tuned to find most of it will necessarily drag in a
large volume of ordinary transactions. The F1 score of 12.54% is what happens
when you average a good recall against a terrible precision, and it is why I
stopped treating F1 as a summary of anything.

The honest read is that **the threshold is a business decision, not a modeling
one**. What a false alarm actually costs — a review queue, a declined card, an
annoyed customer — is not a number the dataset contains, and no amount of
tuning produces it. I could move the threshold to make precision look
respectable and watch recall collapse. Picking a point on that curve requires
knowing something the data does not tell you.

One thing I was glad to see: train AUC of 81.07% against test AUC of 81.51%,
a gap of **-0.43 percentage points**. The model is not overfitting. Whatever
its limitations, they are not the result of memorizing the training set.

## Explaining a score

The IEEE-CIS features are largely anonymized — the dataset ships columns named
`V70`, `C12`, and `M6` with no published definitions — so a model built on them
is opaque by default. That bothered me more than the metrics did.

I added SHAP attribution so every score decomposes into the features that
produced it, moving from a base rate of 46.78% to the final prediction. Across
the test set the heaviest contributors are `V70` and `V69` (Vesta's own
card-activity and device-fingerprint scores), `card6` (debit versus credit),
and `C12` (transactions from the same device).

I also wrote a plain-language glossary for each feature, because a SHAP chart
labeled `V292` explains nothing to a person who has not read the dataset
documentation. Making the output legible turned out to be a bigger share of
the work than training the model was.

## What I learned

- **A single headline metric can hide the whole story.** ROC-AUC near 81% and
  precision near 7% describe the same model.
- **Class imbalance is a framing problem, not just a sampling problem.** The
  useful question was never "how accurate is it" — accuracy is meaningless when
  the positive class is this rare — but "what does each kind of mistake cost."
- **Explainability is not a bonus feature.** A fraud score nobody can
  interrogate is not usable, regardless of its AUC.
- **Deployment surfaced assumptions that training did not.** Serving a
  prediction meant building the feature vector on demand, from inputs shaped
  differently than the training data.

## Where it stands

Complete as a class project. The AWS inference endpoint has since been retired,
so the scoring panel no longer returns live predictions — every metric in the
dashboard is the real trained result, but "Score via AWS" will not respond.
